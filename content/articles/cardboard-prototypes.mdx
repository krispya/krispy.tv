Some ideas get clearer when they are allowed to be flimsy first.

A cardboard prototype is useful precisely because it does not pretend to be finished. It only has to be honest enough to show where the interaction wants to go. In industrial design this is literal — you cut foam-core into phone shapes and hold them in your hand — but for a web interface the equivalent is just as low-tech: a rough component, a handful of fake records, a route that exists mostly so the rest of the system can lean against it.

The trick is resisting the urge to wire things up properly too early. The moment you reach for a real API or a validated schema, the cost of throwing the idea away goes up. And most prototypes _should_ be thrown away. Their job is to absorb the uncertainty so the thing that replaces them can be precise.

I try to keep a rule: if the prototype takes longer than an afternoon, it has already become the product and I have lost the freedom to be wrong.
